X-ray Radiation Effects on SWCNT/PMMA Thin
Film Nanocomposites
Guddi Suman , Member , IEEE, Merlyn Pulikkathara , and Richard Wilkins , Member , IEEE
Abstract—This paper studies the response of a radiation sensor
that was developed from single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT)
and poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) nanocomposite thin ﬁlms
placed on an interdigitated electrode (IDE). We studied the effects
of X-rays in real-time electrical resistance of the thin ﬁlm nanocom-
posites. A 160 kV X-ray source irradiated the nanocomposite
devices with ionizing X-ray radiation at different doses and dose
rates, causing the decrease in resistance of thin ﬁlm through the
production of charge carriers. The embedded SWCNT network
helped transport radiation-induced electrons to the electrodes.
Pre-irradiation resistance can be obtained rapidly from heat treat-
ment of irradiated nanocomposite devices. It is observed that the
required heat treatment time for thin ﬁlm device recovery increased
almost linearly with applied radiation dose. The thin ﬁlm devices
were operated at dc voltage as low as 0.05 V for reliable measure-
ment of the radiation-induced change in electrical resistance. The
results of this study suggest that SWCNT/PMMA thin ﬁlm devices
could be used for sensing X-ray radiation, with signiﬁcantly higher
sensitivity than comparable devices made with multi-walled CNT
(MWCNT) based nanocomposites in a previous study.
Index Terms —Single-walled carbon nanotube, poly (methyl
methacrylate), interdigitated electrode, thin ﬁlm, nanocomposite,
N, N- dimethylformamide, X-ray, radiation detection.

II. M A TERIALS AND METHODS
A. Materials to Make Thin Film Nanocomposite Device
We used the commercial unfunctionalized SWCNT as a nano-
scale ﬁller to prepare thin ﬁlm nanocomposite devices (produced
by a thermal chemical vapor deposition method greater than 95%
purity) were obtained from Nanolab, Inc. with length 1-5 µm
and diameter ∼1.5 nm (D1.5L1-5-S, Nanolab, Inc.). Other ma-
terials used are poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) as a poly-
mer matrix (182230, Sigma Aldrich), N,N-dimethylformamide
(DMF) as solvent (319937, Sigma Aldrich), and acetone for
cleaning purposes obtained from Sigma Aldrich. The purpose
of using DMF for the nanocomposite fabrication process is to
give uniform dispersion of CNTs [17] in the solvent. PMMA
is used during the fabrication of nanocomposite due to its
good spinning qualities [18], high ruggedness [19], and good
chemical resistance [20], solubility in N, N-dimethylformamide
(DMF) [18]. The gold IDEs (#106a Electrodes, Case Western
Reserve University) are deposited on top of a silicon substrate
and are 0.5mm thick. The silicon substrate has dimensions
of 8 mm × 8 mm, with a top layer of 300 nm thick silicon
dioxide. The interdigitated electrode (IDE) and bonding pads
(1.75 mm × 1.75 mm) consist of 200 nm thick gold with a
10 nm thick titanium adhesion layer. The electrodes run parallel
to each other, with a line size spacing of 100 µm.
B. Preparation of SWCNT/PMMA Thin Film
Nanocomposite Device
For the preparation of SWCNT/PMMA nanocomposite ,
0.03g/cm3 concentration of PMMA in DMF was sonicated using
a 750 W, 20 kHz probe sonicator (9810-24-750W, Ace Glass
Inc.) for 15 minutes in an ice bath at 20% amplitude and 0.5 pulse
rate. For the next step, 0.63 mg/cm3 concentration of SWCNT in
DMF was added to the sonicated mixture of PMMA in DMF and
sonicated in the ice bath again for 5 minutes at 20% amplitude
and 0.5 pulse rate. The sonicated mixture of SWCNT/PMMA
and DMF was deposited on the IDE by spin coating the mixture
on the IDE at 1000 rpm with a ramp of 100 rpm/s for 30 seconds
using a spin coater (model 200X, Brewer Science) to create
a conducting SWCNT network atop the IDE gold electrode
pattern, as shown in Fig. 1(a). The thin ﬁlm composite cast on
the IDE was cured in a vacuum oven at 175 °C for one hour
and allowed to cool under a fume hood overnight to remove
excess solvent. This process is also detailed in [Ref. 12]. V ariable

images of the SWCNT network are shown in Fig. 1(b) and of

C. Experimental Setup
A 160 kV , 3000 W X-ray source (X-RAD iR-160) was used to
perform the radiation exposure characterization of the thin ﬁlm
composites of nanomaterial [12]. The X-ray source has an X-ray
tube with a tungsten target-anode at a focal spot of 7.5 mm.
X-ray tube in which an energetic electron beam impinges a
tungsten target, producing an X-ray spectrum that has sharply
deﬁned peaks at 55.55 keV and 66.66 keV with a low-lying broad
bremsstrahlung background [12]. The resulting X-ray beam has
a uniform circular cross-section that is about 15.24 cm in diam-
eter at the surface of the tray in which the thin ﬁlm device was
positioned in the center of the tray. This beam diameter ensured
that all irradiated thin ﬁlm devices were uniformly exposed to the
ionizing X-ray radiation in all experiments reported. The source
to sample distance (SSD) was varied at 30 cm, 50 cm and 70 cm
to study the X-ray radiation dose rate effects on the thin ﬁlm
nanocomposite devices. According to vendor’s speciﬁcation and
calibration, the dose rate at 30 cm SSD is 5.92 krad /min. From
the inverse square law, the calculated X-ray dose rates at 50 cm

A. Total Dose Effect
In this section, the total dose effects were studied by recording
real-time changes in the electrical resistance of the thin ﬁlm
device due to different total doses of X-ray radiation by varying
the radiation exposure time at a constant dose rate. The devices
used in the experiments had initial resistances in the range 2–3
kΩ. The device was placed at 30 cm SSD giving a dose rate
of 5.92 krad/min. The device was irradiated to different X-ray
total doses of 40 krad, 100 krad, 200 krad, 500 krad, 750 krad
and 1000 krad by varying exposure times 7 minutes, 17 min-
utes, 34 minutes, 85 minutes, 128 minutes, and 170 minutes
respectively.
The electrical resistance of thin ﬁlm device and an identical
thin ﬁlm control device (no X-ray exposure) were both recorded
by the semiconductor device analyzer every second interval
for pre-irradiation, during irradiation, and post-irradiation. The
control device was not exposed to radiation. The pre-irradiation
data typically recorded for 10 minutes and post-irradiation data
for 80 minutes. Fig. 2(a) shows a typical data for an experiment.
The resistance of the device substantially decreases continuously
during irradiation for the different total doses, while the resis-
tance of the control sample changes very little (typically less than
0.5%) during the experiment. Fig. 2(b) showed the change in
resistance of SWCNT/PMMA nanocomposite for varying total
doses at a constant dose rate 5.92 krad/min in the bar graph.
The percentage change from values obtained before irradia-
tion is approximately directly proportional to applied radiation
doses. Compared to the initial resistance before exposure, the
thin ﬁlm device that showed the lowest decrease in resistance at
7% was due to a total dose of 40 krad and the highest decrease
in resistance at 58.1% was due to a total dose of 1000 krad.
The post-irradiation data in Figs. 2(a) show that the device did
not immediately recover the initial resistance after X-rays were
turned off. Fig. 2(c) revealed the percentage change in resistance
of thin ﬁlm devices during X-ray exposures with varying X-ray
doses at constant dose rate from the data in Fig. 2(a). The data
points represent the mean percentage change in resistance of
one device for multiple exposures (40 krad, 100 krad, 200 krad,
500 krad, 750 krad, and 1000 krad) with X-rays at dose rate
5.92 krad/min. The vertical bars represent standard deviation.
The experiment was repeated on four devices for multiple
radiation exposures; all showed similar results. Fig. 2(c) showed
that a linear equation could express the relationship between the
change in resistance and X-ray radiation doses. The observed
linear response is important for potential radiation detection
applications of these nanocomposites.

IV . HEA T TREA TMENT ANDRESET
The data in Figs. 2(a) and 3 indicate slow recovery of the
radiation exposed nanocomposite devices. An irradiated device
at 40 krad at ambient conditions slowly regains a resistance
approximately the same as its pre- irradiation value after a few
weeks, which was also observed in [Ref. 12]. Higher recovery
times were observed for nanocomposite devices irradiated at
higher X-rays total doses.
Heat treatment of the device based on nanotube and polymer
reduces the naturally slow and long recovery time [12], [21].
Thus, the irradiated SWCNT/PMMA nanocomposite devices
were subjected to heat treatment at 175 °C in an oven to accel-
erate the device recovery process after irradiation in this study.
The radiated devices at 40 krad, 100 krad, 200 krad and 500 krad
recovered their initial resistance prior to irradiation after heat
treatment. Fig. 5 shows a typical heat treatment recovery of
a device irradiated to 200 krad X-rays at 5.92 krad/min. The
radiated device was heat treated for 45 minutes and then it took
a further 8 minutes to recover the device. Fig. 6 shows the average
heat reset times of the thin ﬁlm device with applied X-ray doses
40 krad, 100 krad, 200 krad, 500 krad, 750 krad, and 1000 krad
at 5.92 krad/min. The heat reset time of SWCNT/PMMA thin
ﬁlm devices increases with increased X-ray total doses.The
increase in X-ray radiation dose increased resistance change,
requiring a longer heat treatment cycle as was seen also with
the MWNT/PMMA, which observed in [Ref. 12]. In this work,
the radiated device at 750 krad and 1000 krad did not fully
recover the resistance to pre-irradiation value, which may be
due to permanent damage of the thin ﬁlm device due to a very
high X-ray dose. Lower dosed, up to 500 krad samples recovered
within about 100% of their pre- irradiated resistance, however,
the higher dosed devices irradiated to 750 krad and 1000 krad
recovered to about 90% of their pre-irradiated resistances.
The radiation exposure and heat treatment experiment was
repeated for eight devices, and similar results were observed in
all cases. The variation in the recovery time of devices was less
than 4%. Fig. 6 shows a clear linear relationship with an R2 value
of 0.9942 between heat reset time and applied X-ray total doses.
V. D ISCUSSION
The decrease in resistance of SWCNT/PMMA nanocompos-
ite thin ﬁlm devices during X-ray exposure was observed, as
shown in Figs. 2(a) and 3. The X-rays cause an ionization
effect through the production of charge carriers and increase the
conductivity of the nanocomposite ﬁlm, similar to that observed
in carbon-ﬁber based devices [22]. The radiation generated
charge carriers are transported to the electrodes by the network of
nanotubes spanning between the electrodes [12]. The MWCNT
network is essential for the charge transport; similar ﬁlms made
from carbon black do not show a signiﬁcant response to X-ray
radiation [12]. The decrease in resistance of SWCNT/PMMA
nanocomposite thin ﬁlm devices (as shown in Figs. 2(a) and (3)
is due to the X-ray irradiation is consistent with the result ob-
served in SWCNT thin ﬁlms device [23] and MWCNT/PMMA
nanocomposite device [12].
Three feature effects were observed in Fig. 3:(1) the resistance
of thin ﬁlms in this work decreases during X-ray exposure,
(2) the rate of change in resistance is directly proportional to
dose rate, and (3) the magnitude of the decrease in resistance
is inversely proportional to X-ray dose rate. These effects were
also observed in the case of MWCNT nanocomposite device
[12]. In this work, the resistance of nanocomposite ﬁlm device
continued decreasing very slowly even after the X-ray is turned
off until it is stable, as shown in Fig. 2(a) and Fig. 3. This is likely
due to the slow release of charges in the insulating polymer near
the SWCNT-polymer interface. Fig. 2(a) and Fig. 3 also showed
the decrease in resistance of less than 0.5% of the control sample
during irradiation, which is very small compared to decrease in
resistance of up to 58.1% due to 1000 krad X-ray irradiation.
The reduction in resistance of the control device may be due
to ambient humidity effects on SWCNTs [2]. Fig. 4 shows that
the decrease in resistance of SWCNT/PMMA thin ﬁlm device
that were operated at the low voltages ranging from 0.05V and
1.00 V , was approximately 13% for all the applied voltages.
However, the result showed more extensive scattered data at
0.05 V compared to a 1.00 V applied voltage, as shown in Fig. 4
is due to increased noise at a very low voltage signal.

In this work, the natural annealing of device at room tempera-
ture is a long process of about several weeks. Fast response due
to high surface area of SWCNTs and low mobility of trapped
holes in the oxide layer of IDE and polymer matrix probably
contribute to the slow recovery of the resistance of thin ﬁlm
after irradiation [12], [25]. Thermal treatment is necessary to
give sufﬁcient energy to trapped holes to free them from such
traps and help irradiated thin ﬁlm nanocomposite to anneal in a
much shorter time [12]. The higher the radiation dose the device
is exposed to, the longer the device needs heat treatment during
the annealing process, as shown in Fig. 6. This is consistent
with the fact that higher doses would generate proportionally
more trapped charges. In addition, the thin ﬁlm devices did not
recover completely their initial resistance after X-ray exposure to
750 krad and 1000 krad. This is likely because higher total X-ray
doses cause destruction and disordering in polymeric structure
[27]. X-ray exposure to nanotube reduces the sp 2 to sp3 nature
of the nanotube bond structure, as well as causing an increase in
defect density, affecting electrical properties of nanotubes [13].
In addition, the heat treatment time for radiated nanocomposite
device at 750 krad and 1000 krad are about 100 minutes and
130 minutes respectively. As the glass transition temperature
of PMMA is 100.01 °C and melting point is 165 °C, heating
SWCNT/PMMA nanocomposite at 175 °C for such longer times
causes decomposition of composites which is associated with
mass loss of PMMA in nanocomposites [26].
In this study, the X-ray radiation effect was studied in
SWCNT/PMMA thin ﬁlm nanocomposite device. A mixed type
SWCNT (semiconducting and metallic) and PMMA was used to
form thin ﬁlm nanocomposite device. The reduction in resistance
may be contributed by the ionization effect on both MWCNT
and PMMA [16].
The data shown in Fig. 7 shows a comparison between
thin ﬁlm nanocomposite devices made from unfunctionalized
SWCNT/PMMA and MWCNT/PMMA(both unfunctionalized
and ferrocene-doped). There is a clear enhanced sensitivity
(as characterized by the resistance change due to X-ray ex-
posure) of unfunctionalized SWCNT/PMMA thin ﬁlm com-
pared with functionalized MWCNT/PMMA as well as unfunc-
tionalized MWCNT/PMMA to X-rays total dose of 100 krad
at dose rate 5.92 krad/min. The overall superior sensitivity
of the SWCNT/PMMA devices is also observed under other
irradiation conditions. The decrease in resistance of unfunc-
tionalized SWCNT/PMMA (Unf-SW) devices during X-ray
exposure in this work was about ﬁve times higher than the
MWCNT/PMMA (Unf-MW) device [12] and about two times
higher than ferrocene-doped MWCNT/PMMA (ferrocene-MW)
[16] due to 100 krad and 5.92 krad/min, as shown in Fig. 7.
The ferrocene-MW data is shown because it had the highest
sensitivity of MWCNT based devices previously studied [16].
The electrical properties of the CNTs network within the ﬁlms
are dependent on the chiralities; this effect is reduced when using
MWCNT. Since the internal tubes are not connected to the outer
tubes in MWCNTs, the contribution of the chirality of the inner
tubes to their electrical properties are diminished and would have
electronic distortion, in comparison to the singular chirality of
SWCNT [24]. In addition, small diameter, larger aspect ratio,
and larger surface area of SWCNTs compared with MWCNTs
could be responsible for more charges produced and transported
in SWCNT ﬁlms compared with MWCNT ﬁlms during radiation
exposure. The morphological difference between SWCNTs and
MWCNTs networks leads to more favorable conditions for
forming crosslinks between SWCNTs than MWCNTs [2], pro-
viding more current paths causing more change in conductivity
in SWCNT based devices during X-ray exposure compared with
MWCNT based devices.
A 3D model to describe the electrical conductivity of CNT
ﬁlled polymer polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) during exposure
to ionizing radiation was studied using the Monte Carlo method
[14]. The researcher used this method to calculate CNT con-
centration, alignment’s inﬂuence on electrical conductivity of
carbon-ﬁlled polymer. A similar model cannot apply in this
study due to several reasons: (1) during the fabrication of
SWCNT/PMMA nanocomposite thin ﬁlm, the sonicated com-
posite was spin-cast on IDE to form thin ﬁlm using a spin
coater, which results in an indeﬁnite number of nanotubes
trapped in thin ﬁlm, (2) SWCNTs used in this study is a
mixed type containing semiconducting and metallic SWCNT
and (3) SWCNT/PMMA is spin-coated on the interdigitated
electrode, so there are a considerably large number of paths be-
tween individual SWCNTs, SWCNTs/PMMA, and SWCNTs/
IDE ﬁnger surface created which increases the conductivity of
the nanocomposite and lowering the overall resistance of the
composite. Further experimental studying may help control the
number of SWCNTs trapped in the desired region and regular
alignment of all SWCNTs across the electrodes.
VI. C ONCLUSION
This study shows that the percentage change in resistance
of thin ﬁlm nanocomposite devices based on SWCNT/PMMA
shows a repeatable quasi-linear response to various applied
total doses and different dose rates of ionizing radiation in the
form of X-rays. The percentage change in resistance of thin
ﬁlm nanocomposites is directly proportional to total dose and

inversely proportional to the dose rate of X-rays which is consis-

the percentage of change in resistance of SWCNT/PMMA com-
posite due to X-rays 100 krad at 1.08 krad/min and 5.92 krad/min

that observed in MWCNT/PMMA [12]. This demonstrates that
composites made from SWCNT/PMMA are more sensitive to
ionizing radiation than those made with MWCNT/PMMA [12].
The devices can be reset with thermal treatment. However, the
thin ﬁlm devices did not recover their initial resistance after 750
and 1000 krad X-rays exposure. The sensors could operate at
low voltage signals with low power consumption, a favorable
result for future operational applications. The results discussed
here show that SWCNT/PMMA thin ﬁlm devices effectively
detect high dose rate X-rays ionizing radiation. Still, improve-
ments for proper instrumentation of the device are necessary
to isolate the device from environmental humidity and other
environment factors that may change device resistance. The dose
rates used in this experiment are comparatively much higher
than those expected in the natural environment. Future work
will include studying the lower dose rates radiation and gamma
radiation effect on nanocomposite devices, which is essential
to evaluate the performance of thin ﬁlm composites for many
applications.
